There's a specific kind of discomfort that comes from reading something you don't fully understand. Not the frustration of bad writing — but the quiet unease of recognising that something important is being said, and not quite being able to grasp it yet.

I keep reading those things anyway.

The instinct to skip ahead

The natural response is to find something easier. Something that confirms what you already know. There's a whole genre of content built around this — explainers that flatten everything into five bullet points, threads that promise you'll "finally understand" some concept in two minutes.

I'm not saying those are bad. But I've noticed that I rarely remember anything from them. The understanding evaporates because it was never really built — it was borrowed.

Confusion as signal

When I read something confusing, I try to treat the confusion as information. What specifically don't I understand? Is it a missing concept, an unfamiliar term, or something the author assumed I knew?

That question — asked honestly — usually points at the exact gap I need to fill. The confusion becomes a map.

The thing about computing

This comes up constantly in computing. The field is built in layers, and every layer assumes you understand the one below. If you skip a layer, you end up with this strange feeling: things work, but you don't really know why.

I'd rather sit with the confusion longer and actually understand it. Even if it takes three times as long.

That's the bet, anyway.
